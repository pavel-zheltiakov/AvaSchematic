1 · Your first schematic

This chapter assumes you have never drawn a circuit and have never used this library. By the end of it you will know the three words the whole thing is built from, and you will have a working schematic on the screen in about fifteen lines. Every term introduced here is in the glossary too.

What a schematic actually is

A schematic is not a picture of a circuit board. It does not say where anything sits, how long a wire is, or how big a part really is. It says one thing only: what is joined to what.

That is why two people can draw the same circuit and get two drawings that look nothing alike but mean exactly the same. A wire drawn as a long detour and a wire drawn as a short hop are the same wire. Moving a part across the sheet changes nothing about the circuit - only about how easy the sheet is to read.

Everything in this library follows from that. The drawing is stored as things that are joined; where the lines actually run is worked out afterwards, and worked out again whenever you move something.

Three words

  • Part - a resistor, a transistor, a box on an architecture diagram. In the API a part is a SchematicNode: a placement of a drawing.
  • Pin - the spot on a part where a wire is allowed to attach. A resistor has two, a transistor three, a chip forty. In the API a pin is a Port, and it has a name like "1" or "B".
  • Wire - a join between two pins. In the API it is a SchematicConnection, and it knows the two ends it joins, not the path between them.

The control

SchematicView is an ordinary Avalonia control. Put it in a window like any other. 

<Window xmlns:sch="clr-namespace:AvaSchematic.Controls;assembly=AvaSchematic">
    <sch:SchematicView x:Name="View" />
</Window>

It has no content, no items panel and no templates. It owns a document and paints it, which is why one control can show a sheet with ten thousand parts on it without ten thousand visuals existing.

Fifteen lines

A SymbolLibrary is a box of drawings with names on them. CreateDefault() gives you the built-in one: eighty parts and shapes, electronics and diagrams together. A SchematicDocument is the sheet. 

using AvaSchematic.Model;
using AvaSchematic.Symbols;
using AvaSchematic.Symbols.Libraries;

var library  = SymbolLibrary.CreateDefault();
var document = new SchematicDocument(library) { GridSize = 10 };

// A resistor, placed so that its pin "1" lands exactly on (0, 0).
var r1 = document.AddNodeAtPin(ElectronicSymbols.ResistorIec, "1", new Point(0, 0));
r1.Designator = "R1";
r1.Value      = "10k";

// A ground symbol, 100 units below it.
var gnd = document.AddNodeAtPin(ElectronicSymbols.Ground, "1", new Point(0, 100));

// Join the resistor's other end to it.
document.Connect(r1, "2", gnd, "1");

View.Document = document;
View.ZoomToFit();

That is a complete, correct schematic: a 10k resistor to ground. Run it and you will see the resistor, its designator and value beside it, the ground symbol, and a line joining them - a line nobody drew.

Why AddNodeAtPin and not AddNode

Both exist. AddNode places a part by the top-left corner of its box, which is what you want for a diagram box you are about to type a caption into.

AddNodeAtPin places it by one of its pins instead, and in a schematic that is nearly always the thing you actually know. You know the net should run down the left of the sheet at x = 0; you do not know or care that this particular resistor symbol's box starts 30 units to the left of its first pin. Place by pin and every pin lands on the grid, whatever the symbol happens to be and however it is turned.

What you have

A document, two parts and a wire. Nothing here said where the line should run, and that is the point: the next chapter is about the parts, and the one after that is about how the line found its way.
