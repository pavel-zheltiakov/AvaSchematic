Glossary

Every word the rest of this documentation uses, in plain English. The reference links here: a term you have not met in a type's description is a link, and it lands on the entry for it. Each entry says what the thing is, what it is called in this API, and which chapter teaches it properly.

The drawing

Schematic

A drawing that says what is joined to what. It does not say where anything sits in real life, how long a wire is, or how big a part really is - two schematics that look nothing alike can mean exactly the same circuit. Moving a part changes how easy the sheet is to read and nothing else.

In AvaSchematic: SchematicDocument · chapter 1

Symbol

The drawing of a kind of part: the rectangle that means resistor, the triangle that means op-amp. There is one symbol for resistors, and a thousand resistors on a sheet all point at it.

In AvaSchematic: SymbolDefinition, SymbolLibrary · chapter 2

Pin

A spot on a part where a wire may attach: a resistor's two ends, a transistor's base, collector and emitter. A pin has a key you wire by, and a direction it faces - which is why wires leave pins straight instead of bending at them.

In AvaSchematic: Port · chapter 2

Port

The same thing as a pin. "Pin" is what an electronics drawing calls it; "port" is what a diagram calls the little dot on the edge of a box. One type covers both, which is why the class is called Port and the documentation says "pin" whenever it is talking about a circuit.

In AvaSchematic: Port · chapter 2

Designator

The short name that says which part this is: R1, C4, U2, Q7. The letter says what kind of part it is, the number makes it unique on the sheet. Its full name is reference designator, and it is the handle everything downstream - the parts list, the board, the person soldering - uses.

In AvaSchematic: SchematicNode.Designator, AttributeKeys · chapter 2

Footprint

The physical pattern of copper the part will be soldered to. It has nothing to do with the schematic and everything to do with what happens after it, so it rides along as one more field on the part.

In AvaSchematic: node["Footprint"] · chapter 2

Bill of materials

The shopping list that falls out of a finished sheet: every part, its designator, its value, and how many of each. Nothing in this library produces one for you - it is a loop over document.Nodes, and what belongs in it differs from shop to shop.

In AvaSchematic: document.Nodes · chapter 7

The circuit

Net

Everything joined together electrically. Several wires, the dots between them and all the pins they reach are one net: if you can walk from one pin to another along lines, they are on the same one. You never build a net - you draw wires, and the nets fall out of them.

In AvaSchematic: Net · chapter 4

Netlist

The list of every net and the pins on it - the circuit as data rather than as a picture. It is what you hand to a simulator or a board layout tool, and it is the only output of a schematic that a machine downstream actually reads.

In AvaSchematic: NetlistExtractor, Netlist · chapter 4

Junction, junction dot

The filled dot drawn where lines of one net actually join. No dot means the lines merely cross and are not joined - which is why a missing dot is the classic way to publish a circuit that cannot work. Three or more distinct directions leaving one point, with at least one line starting, ending or turning there, is what earns a dot.

In AvaSchematic: SchematicJunction, JunctionResolver · chapter 4

Ground

The reference every voltage is measured against, drawn as a little stack of shrinking bars. Every ground symbol on a sheet is the same net even though no wire joins them, which is what saves a schematic from being a plate of spaghetti.

In AvaSchematic: ElectronicSymbols.Ground · chapter 4

Power rail

The same idea as ground, for a supply: a small symbol labelled +5V or +3V3. Every one carrying the same label is one net, wherever it is drawn.

In AvaSchematic: ElectronicSymbols.PowerRail · chapter 4

Net label

A name written on a wire. Two wires carrying the same net label are one net with no line drawn between them - the way a signal gets from one corner of a sheet to the other without a wire crossing everything in between.

In AvaSchematic: ElectronicSymbols.NetLabel · chapter 4

Bus

Several related signals drawn as one thick line - D0 to D7 shown as "D[0..7]". A drawing convention for keeping a sheet readable, not a different kind of connection.

In AvaSchematic: a wire with a Label and a thicker Style · chapter 4

The lines

Orthogonal

Right angles only: every piece of a line runs either across or down, never diagonally. Also called Manhattan routing, after the street grid. It is what makes a schematic readable, and it is the default.

In AvaSchematic: RouteMode.Orthogonal, OrthogonalRouter · chapter 3

Stub

The short straight piece of wire that leaves a pin before the first bend. Without it a wire looks like it is passing behind the part rather than connecting to it. The end of the stub is put on a grid line, so wires leaving different parts share lanes instead of missing each other by a few units.

In AvaSchematic: SchematicConnection.Clearance · chapter 3

Lane

One of the parallel tracks, a grid step apart, that a wire may run along. Two wires never share one, because a reader could not tell where one ended and the other began. Crossing is fine; running together is not.

In AvaSchematic: SchematicRules.LaneSpacing, MinParallelGap · chapter 3

Obstacle

Something a line must not be drawn across - in practice, the body of a part it is not wired to. A line through a part looks exactly like a line connected to it, so the router goes round.

In AvaSchematic: SchematicRules.AvoidSymbols, SchematicNode.BlocksRouting · chapter 3

Waypoint

A corner you placed by hand. A line with waypoints goes through them instead of working its own way out, which is what happens the moment you drag one of its runs.

In AvaSchematic: SchematicConnection.Waypoints, SegmentEditor · chapter 3
