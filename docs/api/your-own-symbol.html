6 · Drawing your own part

Sooner or later the part you need is not in the box. A symbol is a small drawing plus a list of pins, and the builder reads like instructions for drawing it. This chapter makes one from scratch.

The symbol's own coordinates

A symbol is drawn around its own origin, in its own units, and knows nothing about where it will end up. Put the middle of the part at (0, 0) and the pins where they belong relative to it. When the part is placed, moved, turned or mirrored, one matrix carries the whole drawing with it.

Keep the pin tips on a round number - 10 units is the usual pitch - and every instance will land on the grid however it is turned.

A relay, in six lines

var relay = SymbolBuilder.Create("my/relay", "Relay")
    .Category("Electromechanical")
    .FixedSize()                                     // a part keeps its pin pitch
    .Rect(-20, -25, 40, 50).NoFill()                   // the body
    .Line(-20, -10, 20, -10)                        // the coil divider
    .Pin("A1", -40,  10, PortSide.Left,  20, name: "COIL+", number: "1")
    .Pin("A2",  40,  10, PortSide.Right, 20, name: "COIL-", number: "2")
    .Attribute(AttributeKeys.Designator, "K?", 0, -30,
               hAlign: TextHAlign.Center, vAlign: TextVAlign.Bottom)
    .Build();

library.Register(relay);
document.AddNodeAtPin("my/relay", "A1", new Point(0, 0));

Reading it back: a rectangle 40 by 50 centred on the origin, a line across it, a pin sticking out 20 units to the left whose tip - the end a wire attaches to - is at (-40, 10), another to the right, and a designator that starts as "K?" and sits 30 units above the middle.

What a pin argument means

  • key - what you wire with: Connect(k1, "A1", …).
  • tipX, tipY - the outer end, where a wire lands.
  • side - which way the pin faces. This is what makes a wire leave it straight instead of sideways, so it matters more than it looks.
  • length - how long the little lead is, drawn inwards from the tip.
  • name and number - the signal name shown inside the body, and the physical leg shown outside it. Give either and it is shown; leave both out and neither is.

A chip from nothing but a pin list

The usual way an application turns a datasheet into a symbol: give it the left and right pin names and it draws the box, spaces the pins, numbers them and labels them. The pin keys come out as "L0", "L1", "R0" and so on, down each side. 

var mcu = ElectronicSymbols.CreateIc("my/stm32g031", "STM32G031",
    leftPins:  new[] { "VDD", "VSS", "NRST", "PA0", "PA1" },
    rightPins: new[] { "PA2", "PA3", "PB0", "SWDIO", "SWCLK" },
    bodyWidth: 120);

library.Register(mcu);

Fixed or stretchable

.FixedSize() means the drawing is the size it is. Every electronic part is fixed, because a resistor stretched to fit a box would have its pins off the grid.

.Stretchable() means the drawing is squeezed into whatever box the node is given, which is what a diagram shape wants: an architecture box has to grow to fit its caption.

Keeping your symbols

A library saves and loads as JSON, and a document can carry the symbols it used inside itself, so a sheet opens on a machine that has never heard of your parts. 

File.WriteAllText("parts.json", SchematicJson.SaveLibrary(library));
var loaded = SchematicJson.LoadLibrary(File.ReadAllText("parts.json"));

var json = SchematicJson.Save(document,
    new SchematicJson.SaveOptions { EmbedSymbols = true });
