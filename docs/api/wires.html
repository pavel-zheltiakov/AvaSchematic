3 · Wires

You say which two pins are joined. The library decides where the line runs, keeps it out of other lines' way, and takes it round anything solid. This chapter is about how that works and what you can say to change it.

Joining two pins

document.Connect(r1, "2", q1, "B");

That is the whole of it. The SchematicConnection stores two endpoints, and an endpoint can be four things:

  • a pin - the usual one, and the only one a real schematic uses;
  • a whole shape - the line sticks to the outline and slides round it as either box is dragged. This is what diagram tools call a dynamic connector;
  • a free point - a loose end at a fixed spot on the sheet;
  • another line - a tap onto an existing wire, which is how a branch joins a net.
var link = new SchematicConnection(
    ConnectionEndpoint.ToShape(apiBox),
    ConnectionEndpoint.ToShape(dbBox)) { EndArrow = ArrowHead.Arrow, Label = "SQL" };
document.Add(link);

The shape of a line

RouteMode says what kind of line it is:

  • Orthogonal - right angles only, worked out for you. What a schematic uses.
  • Direct - a straight line between the ends.
  • Curved - a smooth curve, for mindmaps.
  • OrthogonalManual and Polyline - right angles, or straight segments, through the corners you gave it. This is what a line becomes the moment you drag part of it by hand.

You can also hand a line a list of waypoints and it will go through them.

Why a wire leaves a pin straight

Look at any schematic and no wire ever leaves a pin sideways: it comes straight out for a little way first, and only then turns. That short straight piece is a stub, and it is what makes a pin readable - a line that bends at the pin looks like it is passing behind the part rather than connecting to it.

Every pin knows which way it faces, so the router pushes a stub out along that direction before it starts looking for a path. It also lands the end of that stub on a grid line, which matters more than it sounds: two wires that leave two different parts then run in the same lanes instead of a few units apart, and a reader can tell them apart.

Two lines never share a lane

If two wires run alongside each other on the same line, a reader cannot tell where one ends and the other begins. So they never do. When a route would sit on top of an existing one, the search steps sideways into the next free lane - one grid step over - and takes that instead.

Crossing is fine. Two lines that cross at a right angle are unambiguous, and a schematic is full of them. Running together is what is banned.

Lines go round parts

A line drawn across the middle of a transistor looks exactly like a line connected to it. So every part on the sheet is solid as far as routing is concerned, and a wire goes round.

Anything the line is actually wired to is exempt, of course - a wire has to reach its own pins. So are container shapes: a boundary or a package exists precisely so that things sit inside it, and treating one as solid would make everything in it unroutable. That is worked out automatically, and any single part can be opted out with node.BlocksRouting = false.

Why the sheet does not shuffle

Every wire is laid out in one pass, in document order, and each one only avoids the wires laid out before it. Nothing can chase anything else round the sheet, and the same document always produces the same drawing. There is more about this in How a sheet is laid out.

When you want it your way

Grab the little diamond in the middle of any run of a selected line and slide it. The line stops being automatic at that moment - it keeps the corners you gave it - and the runs either side stretch to follow. A run touching a pin grows a jog rather than dragging the wire off the pin. 

SegmentEditor.Materialize(wire);   // freeze the current automatic route into corners
wire.Waypoints.Add(new Point(120, 40));
wire.Mode = RouteMode.OrthogonalManual;
