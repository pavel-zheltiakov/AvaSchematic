4 · Dots and nets

Two lines crossing on a sheet may or may not be joined, and the drawing has to say which. This chapter is about the little black dot that says it, and about getting the answer - what is joined to what - back out as data.

A net

Everything electrically joined together is one net. Three wires, a junction and six pins can all be one net; the drawing has many lines but the circuit has one node.

You never build a net. You draw wires, and the net falls out of them: two pins are on the same net if you can get from one to the other by following lines. Working that out is one call. 

var netlist = new NetlistExtractor().Extract(document);

foreach (var net in netlist.Nets)
    Console.WriteLine(net);            // N3: R7.2, Q1.B, C4.1

foreach (var loose in netlist.UnconnectedTerminals)
    Console.WriteLine($"nothing reaches {loose.Reference}");

A net that reaches a net label, a ground or a power rail takes its name from it - GND, +5V, PWM - and anything else gets a number. Two grounds drawn on opposite corners of the sheet with no wire between them are still one net, because they carry the same name. That is the whole point of the ground symbol: it saves you drawing a wire to everything.

When a dot is drawn

A junction dot means these lines are joined here. No dot means they merely cross. Getting this wrong is the classic way to publish a circuit that cannot work, so the rule is strict and the drawing follows it by itself:

The rule the library applies is: count the distinct directions lines of one net leave the point in, and require at least one of them to actually start, end or turn there. Three or more, and it is a junction. A corner is only two directions, so no dot. A pin with one wire arriving is a terminal, not a branch, so no dot - unless you ask for that style, which some houses use. 

document.Rules.JunctionMinBranches = 3;      // the default
document.Rules.CountPinsAsBranches = true;   // dot every terminal, if that is your style
document.Rules.JunctionOnCrossing  = true;   // only for diagrams where a crossing really is a join

Reading the drawing back to you

RuleChecker walks the finished sheet and reports what does not obey the rulebook - two lines running together, a line across a part, a junction with more branches than good practice allows. 

foreach (var problem in new RuleChecker().Check(document))
    Console.WriteLine(problem.Message);
// A line runs 42 units across Q1 near (180, -5).

Each report carries the two items it is about, so a host can select them and show the user rather than printing a sentence.
