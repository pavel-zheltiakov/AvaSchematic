Schematics that draw their own wires.

A control for Avalonia that draws electronic schematics — and mindmaps, flowcharts, UML and architecture diagrams, because underneath they are the same thing. You say which pins are joined; it works out where every line runs, keeps two lines out of one lane, takes them round the parts, and puts a dot exactly where three of them meet.

Run the demo git clone https://github.com/pavel-zheltiakov/AvaSchematic.git

This is a beta. The drawing, the routing and the file format work; the API may still move before 12.1.0 final.

The same control, six ways

What it does for you

Wires route themselves

You say which two pins are joined. Right angles, a straight stub out of every pin, the fewest bends that will do, and a fresh route the moment anything moves.

Two lines never share a lane

Wires that would run alongside each other step into the next free lane instead. Crossing is fine; running together is what a reader cannot untangle.

Lines go round parts

A line across a transistor looks exactly like a line connected to it. Parts are solid, and when no elbow will fit past one, a search finds the way round.

Dots that mean something

A dot is drawn where three or more directions of one net meet — and nowhere else. A crossing gets none, a corner gets none, a pin with one wire gets none.

The circuit, as data

One call gives you the net list: every net and the pins on it, named after the ground, rail or net label it reaches. One more call checks the sheet against its own rules.

Editing that behaves

Drag a part and the wires follow. Drag one run of a wire and the runs either side stretch. Drag a designator and it stays with its part. Every gesture is one undo step.

Nothing is a control

The whole sheet is painted in one pass with no visual per item, so ten thousand parts cost what ten do. Zoom is a matrix, not a re-layout.

One file, no surprises

Plain JSON that holds the drawing and, if you ask, the symbols it used — so a sheet opens on a machine that has never heard of your parts.

A whole schematic, in fifteen lines

var library  = SymbolLibrary.CreateDefault();
var document = new SchematicDocument(library) { GridSize = 10 };

var r1 = document.AddNodeAtPin(Symbols.Electric.ResistorIEC, "1", new Point(0, 0));
r1.Designator = "R1";
r1.Value      = "10k";

var gnd = document.AddNodeAtPin(Symbols.Electric.GND, "1", new Point(0, 100));
document.Connect(r1, "2", gnd, "1");

View.Document = document;
View.ZoomToFit();

AddNodeAtPin places a part by one of its pins rather than by its bounding box, which is the thing you actually know when you are drawing a circuit. Chapter one walks through every line of this.

What it does not do

Worth knowing before you install it rather than after.

  • It is not an EDA package. There is no board layout, no simulation, no part database and no design-rule check beyond the drawing conventions. It draws sheets and tells you what is joined to what; what you do with that is yours.
  • Hierarchical sheets are not built in. One document is one sheet. Nets are named across a sheet by labels, rails and grounds, and joining sheets together is the host's job.
  • Buses are a drawing convention, not a type. A thick labelled line is a line with a label and a thicker stroke; nothing expands D[0..7] into eight nets for you.
  • Avoidance has a ceiling. Past six hundred wires on one sheet the overlap and obstacle passes switch off, because beyond that a stacked line is cheaper than a frozen editor. The number is a rule you can change.
  • A part is not a control. The sheet is painted, not composed, so you cannot put an Avalonia button inside a resistor. Panels, menus and dialogs live outside the surface as usual.
